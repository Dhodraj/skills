# Durable decisions: why each input matters

## The central idea

A **durable decision does not change while its inputs do not change.** It prevents one failure: a decision is made, and over the following weeks it unmakes itself. Nobody reopens it on purpose. The inputs drifted or were never pinned down, and the team slid back toward the rejected path. By the time anyone notices, neither option was carried out well.

The fix is to pin four inputs so firmly that the decision reopens only when one of them changes materially.

## The four inputs

**1. Goals.** Start here: disagreement at this stage causes the most rework later. Be precise about goals and non-goals, and classify each goal: result-bound (done when a metric reaches a threshold), delivery-bound (done when something ships) or time-bound (done by a date). Write them down.

**2. Assumptions.** Options carry assumptions about time, money, technical capacity (compute, memory, disk, network) and people (headcount, bandwidth). Assumptions are the second-largest cause of decisions collapsing, after unclear goals: people assumed different things, or the assumptions changed. Recording every assumption wastes time; record the major ones, those likely to break or to cause disagreement.

**3. Options.** List the real choices, their trade-offs and the reason for preferring one. The hard part is not getting attached. The test: you do not understand an opposing argument until you can argue its points, just as strongly, for its champion, with the champion not in the room. Only when you can steelman each option have you understood the choice.

**4. Decider(s).** Name who makes the call, a person or a committee, so that when circumstances change, people know whom to talk to.

## Making it stick

Consult the people the decision affects early, while options and assumptions are still forming, not after the call. Write the decision where everyone can see it. Future teammates will ask why the system does X.

## Disagree and commit

A durable decision reopens only when an input changes, so people can disagree and still commit fully: they know it will be revisited if and only if circumstances change. If deciders let a decision reopen without new input, people stop believing decisions are final and invest less in making them well. The hardest calls are usually the closest ones; knowing they reopen when inputs change lets everyone get behind them.
